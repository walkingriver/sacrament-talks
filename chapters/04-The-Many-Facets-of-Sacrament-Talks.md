# The Many Facets of Sacrament Talks

In the church's _General Handbook,_ you will see this brief overview of
sacrament meeting talks:

> The bishopric selects speakers for sacrament meeting. Most often they
> invite ward members, including youth...
>
> The bishopric extends invitations to speak well in advance of the
> meeting. Speakers bear testimony of Jesus Christ and teach His gospel
> using the scriptures. Messages should build faith and be consistent
> with the sacred nature of the sacrament.\
> \
> https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/general-handbook/29-meetings-in-the-church?lang=eng&id=p32-p33#p32

That isn't much guidance for someone preparing to speak, is it? The handbook tells us what a talk should accomplish, but not how to get there. Let's explore the different types of talks you might be asked to give, and how to approach each one effectively.

## Informational

An informational talk teaches the audience about a specific topic or concept. These talks provide knowledge that members can apply in their own lives - whether it's church doctrine, scriptural insights, or practical gospel principles. The key is to present clear, focused information while keeping your audience engaged.

### Example

Elder Quentin L. Cook's October 2018 General Conference talk, "Deep and Lasting Conversion to Heavenly Father and the Lord Jesus Christ," shows how to deliver important information effectively. When explaining the new Sunday meeting schedule, he keeps it clear and straightforward:

> The Sunday Church meetings will consist of a 60-minute sacrament
> meeting each Sunday, focused on the Savior, the ordinance of the
> sacrament, and spiritual messages. After time for transition to
> classes, Church members will attend a 50-minute class that will
> alternate each Sunday:

Notice how he presents the information clearly while maintaining the sacred nature of the subject. When giving an informational talk, remember:
- Focus on one main concept rather than trying to cover too much
- Use concrete examples that relate to daily life
- Share how this knowledge has blessed your own life
- Keep the tone respectful while being engaging

## Inspirational

Inspirational talks, perhaps the most common in sacrament meeting, aim to uplift and encourage. Your role isn't to lecture, but to share insights that might help others in their own spiritual journey. President Thomas S. Monson was a master of this approach, as shown in his April 2008 talk, "Finding Joy in the Journey":

> Let us relish life as we live it, find joy in the journey, and share
> our love with friends and family. One day, each of us will run out of
> tomorrows.

When giving an inspirational talk, consider the following:
- Share personal experiences that illustrate the principle you're teaching
- Use stories and anecdotes to make the message more relatable
- Emphasize the importance of applying the principle in daily life
- End with a call to action or a challenge to the audience

## Entertaining

While the primary goal of an entertaining talk is to engage the audience, it's essential to consider the context in which you're speaking. Strive to be engaging, but recognize that attempting to entertain may not always be appropriate, depending on the setting. A majority of talks can incorporate some level of entertainment, and most presentations should be engaging in one way or another. This doesn't mean every talk must be humorous; often, serious talks can be just as powerful. However, adding a touch of humor or other forms of entertainment can make even the most serious talks more engaging.

In an October 2008 General Conference talk titled "Come What May, and Love It," Elder Joseph B. Wirthlin related an amusing story about one of his daughter's blind dates:

> She was all dressed up and waiting for her date to arrive when the
> doorbell rang. In walked a man who seemed a little old, but she tried
> to be polite... We watched as she got into the car, but the car didn't
> move. Eventually our daughter got out of the car and, red faced, ran
> back into the house. The man that she thought was her blind date had
> actually come to pick up another of our daughters who had agreed to be
> a babysitter for him and his wife.

This light-hearted story demonstrates how humor can be used to make a talk more engaging and entertaining, even when discussing serious matters. When incorporating entertainment into your talk, remember:
- Know your audience and tailor your approach accordingly
- Use humor or other forms of entertainment to illustrate a point or make the message more relatable
- Avoid using entertainment as a way to avoid the main message or principle
- Keep the tone respectful and avoid offending or alienating any members of the audience

## Testimony

Sharing your personal testimony allows you to express your faith in the principles, doctrines, or experiences you're discussing. A sincere testimony demonstrates your conviction and can inspire others to reflect on their own beliefs and experiences. As you share your testimony, focus on the core aspects of your faith, such as your belief in Jesus Christ, the restored gospel, or the power of prayer. You can also share specific experiences where you felt God's guidance or witnessed His hand in your life. Remember to speak from the heart, as genuine emotions can create a lasting impact on your audience.

Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, in his October 2013 General Conference talk titled "Like a Broken Vessel," shares a powerful and personal testimony of the Church and the Savior. Here's a quote from that talk:

> I testify of the holy Resurrection, that unspeakable cornerstone gift
> in the Atonement of the Lord Jesus Christ! With the Apostle Paul, I
> testify that that which was sown in corruption will one day be raised
> in incorruption and that which was sown in weakness will ultimately be
> raised in power. I bear witness of that day when loved ones whom we
> knew to have disabilities in mortality will stand before us glorified
> and grand, breathtakingly perfect in body and mind. What a thrilling
> moment that will be!

In this quote, Elder Holland bears witness to the Atonement and Resurrection of Jesus Christ and the promise of perfect healing that it offers to all who suffer from disabilities and challenges in this life. When sharing your testimony, remember:
- Speak from the heart and be genuine
- Focus on the core aspects of your faith
- Share specific experiences that illustrate your testimony
- Avoid using your testimony as a way to lecture or persuade others

## Bringing It All Together

Most effective sacrament meeting talks will incorporate elements from several of these categories. You might begin with an informational foundation, weave in personal experiences and appropriate stories to illustrate your points, and conclude with a heartfelt testimony. The key is to maintain a balance that serves your message while respecting the sacred nature of the meeting.

Remember that these categories aren't rigid rules, but rather frameworks to help you organize your thoughts and approach your assignment effectively. As you prepare your talk, focus first on the message you've been asked to share, then consider which of these approaches will best help you convey that message to your ward family.
